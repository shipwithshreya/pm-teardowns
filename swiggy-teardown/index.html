Thesis

The opportunity isn't to make Swiggy do more. It's to make every commitment feel more certain.

The question I set out to answer: how might Swiggy reduce friction across price, reliability and discovery without compromising the convenience of its core food-ordering experience?

Lens
Certainty at the moments a user commits
Surface
Consumer app: food ordering flow and ecosystem entry points
Method
Observable product behavior + product principles

Context

What is Swiggy, and who is it for?

A food app that became a platform of everyday decisions.

Swiggy started with restaurant delivery and now spans several services. Each asks for a different kind of commitment, from a routine dinner to a planned night out.

The Routine User

Orders often. The flow should feel automatic.

NeedsPredictable pricing, reliable delivery, low-friction reordering

The Time-Sensitive User

Needs food by a particular time, or can't cook.

NeedsCertainty, reliable fulfillment, accurate estimates

02
Problem

What problem are we solving?

A refund resolves the transaction. It doesn't restore the need.

A refund can return the money. It can't return the time, convenience or certainty the user lost. Of the three friction points I found, this one costs the user most, so it leads.

Observed

Swiggy already surfaces some delay risk before ordering, such as Order may take longer than usual during weather or delivery-delay conditions.

The gap Hypothesis

That messaging covers lateness. Fulfillment confidence could extend to other failure points, especially an order that fails or is cancelled after the user has committed.

Why it hurts

  • The user is hungry, or ordering around a planned meal time
  • They've already invested time choosing
  • They may be ordering for several people
  • Plans are hard to change at the last minute
  • A failure means restarting the search
Product question

How might Swiggy prevent predictable fulfillment failures instead of relying primarily on post-failure recovery?

I have no data on how often orders fail or get cancelled after acceptance, or why. I treat post-commit cancellation as a meaningful failure scenario to validate, not a measured Swiggy-wide rate. I'm not blaming restaurants or delivery partners; many causes are systemic (demand spikes, stock running out, weather, distance).

Two other friction points

Transparency · before payment

The total is visible. What makes it up takes more effort to understand.

Observed

Item prices and the final payable amount are both visible. Understanding exactly what contributes to the total (delivery, platform fee, taxes, other charges) can require additional interaction.

Why it matters Hypothesis

Users anchor on item prices while browsing, and price-sensitive users compare totals. When the gap between the two is hard to read, the amount feels less predictable, even when every charge is fair.

Product question

How might Swiggy make the amount the user is about to commit to easier to understand and predict, without unnecessarily sacrificing fee revenue?

Discovery · across the ecosystemHypothesis

High-frequency services may crowd out lower-frequency ones.

My hypothesis

As Swiggy expands beyond food, routine destinations may compete with occasional ones for the same screen space. Permanent placement rewards what people need often; a table booking or an event matters on a few specific evenings.

Why it matters

Promote everything and the core ordering flow gets cluttered. Promote nothing and newer services rely on users finding them alone.

Product question

How should Swiggy distribute attention across its ecosystem without compromising the core ordering experience?

03
Insight

What connects the three problems?

The biggest opportunity isn't adding features. It's reducing uncertainty before and after the user commits.

These aren't three random feature ideas. They're three forms of the same uncertainty: the user commits now and finds out later.

Transparency

“What exactly am I committing to pay?”

The total is visible; what makes it up takes effort to read.
Reliability · Priority #1

“Will I actually get what I ordered?”

Confirmed only after the alternatives are gone.
Discovery

“Is this relevant to what I want to do right now?”

Left for the user to work out.

The fix in each case is to move certainty earlier, to the point where the user can still act on it.

04
Priority

Why reliability first?

RICE says one thing. Strategy says another.

I scored the three opportunities with RICE to force explicit reasoning, then made the call where the score falls short.

What RICE says: transparency first

Illustrative scores · my judgment

ReliabilityFulfillment confidence3350%31.5
TransparencyCheckout transparency3180%12.4
DiscoveryContextual discovery2140%20.4

RICE = Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort. Every input is a directional judgment relative to the others, not a Swiggy metric.

What strategy says

I would prioritize reliability.

  1. Severity doesn't average out.RICE averages impact across everyone reached. Reliability failures are rare, severe events, and one bad night can end the habit.
  2. It protects the core delivery promise.Clear pricing and better discovery matter less if the order doesn't arrive as expected.
  3. It builds a lasting capability.Once failures can be predicted, the same signal can help with ETAs, restaurant ranking and ops planning.

The score shapes the sequencing, not the bet. Transparency ships first as a quick win; fulfillment confidence gets the main investment.

#1 · Main bet

Reliability: fulfillment confidence

Addresses the deepest trust problem and prevents the failure instead of compensating for it. Start with a narrow pilot to test calibration.

#2 · Quick win

Transparency: checkout

High reach, low complexity. Ships in parallel without competing for the same team.

#3 · Explore

Discovery: contextual

Potential ecosystem value, lower urgency. Validate one trigger first.

05
Solution

What would I change?

One intervention per problem, in priority order.

Each one moves information to a point where the user can still act on it.

Solution 1 · ReliabilityPriority #1Recommendation

Tell users how likely an order is to succeed before they place it.

Compensating for failure→Preventing avoidable failure

Observed Swiggy already warns when an order may take longer than usual. I'd build on that, not replace it.

Don't just warn the user about risk. Give them a viable alternative while they can still act.

  • Extend confidence beyond delayCover other failure points too, especially an order likely to fail or be cancelled after the user commits.
  • A clean, non-alarmist messageOnly when signals are reliable enough: a short “Lower fulfillment confidence” note before checkout. Silent otherwise.
  • A viable alternative, right thereContinue with this order, or see similar items from nearby restaurants with higher confidence.

06
Measurement

How would we know it worked?

Measure the promise, not the apology.

I would measure whether orders arrive as promised, not how well failures are compensated.

North Star · Fulfillment confidence

Successful orders fulfilled as promised

Definition. Orders delivered complete, within the promised window, without cancellation, as a share of orders placed.

It counts what the user counts. An accepted order that later fails doesn't count.

Prevention Did failures drop?

  • Cancellation rateAfter the order is placed, split by who cancelled and why
  • Late-delivery rateDelivered past the promised window
  • Missing or incomplete-item rateOrders with at least one missing item
  • Order completion ratePlaced orders that reach the user

Signal quality Is the prediction honest?

  • CalibrationOf orders shown high confidence, the share fulfilled as promised
  • False-alarm rateLower-confidence messages on orders that were fulfilled as promised
  • Switch outcomeDid users who switched get a better result?

Recovery What did failure cost?

  • Refund rateRead alongside prevention metrics, never alone
  • Support contacts per orderEspecially “where is my order” and missing items

Retention & guardrails Did trust hold?

  • Repeat ordering after an incidentThe best test of whether trust survived
  • Cart → order conversionGuardrail: the signal shouldn't scare off good orders
  • Order volume for flagged restaurantsGuardrail: don't unfairly penalize a busy patch
The trap

Don't optimize only for refunds.

A lower refund rate could mean fewer failures. It could also mean:

  • Refunds became harder to claim
  • Users gave up reporting problems
  • Failures shifted to forms refunded less, such as lateness

So I'd pair every recovery metric with a fulfillment metric and a retention metric. If refunds fall but repeat ordering after incidents doesn't improve, the experience didn't improve.

Checkout transparency Supporting

  • Checkout abandonmentEspecially at the bill summary step
  • Price-related support contactsQuestions about charges
  • Cart → payment conversionWatch for a short-term dip as the breakdown gets more prominent
  • Repeat orderingDoes predictability build habit?

Contextual discovery Supporting

  • Engagement with promptsTap-through and dismissal rates
  • Cross-surface discovery rateUsers trying a second service for the first time
  • Downstream conversionBookings or tickets that follow a prompt
  • Repeat use of discovered surfacesDid discovery become a habit?

No baselines or targets, on purpose: setting them needs Swiggy's internal data. The first step would be measuring each metric's current baseline before any test.

07
Trade-offs

What could go wrong?

Risks, and how I'd guard against them.

Reliability

Fulfillment confidence

  • A poorly calibrated signal creates unnecessary caution.Pilot in a limited area, track false alarms, warn only above a tested threshold.
  • Alternatives must be better, not just available.Recommend only higher-confidence options with similar cuisine and price.
  • Predictions degrade during unusual demand spikes.Fall back to honest, wider ETAs rather than false precision.
Transparency

Checkout transparency

  • A clearer breakdown makes price changes more visible.Accept it. Changes the user can understand cost less trust over time.
  • Transparency doesn't mean lower fees.Set expectations internally: more predictability, not lower prices.
  • Too much detail adds checkout friction.Total up front, detail one tap away.
Discovery

Contextual discovery

  • More prompts can become clutter.Hard frequency caps and back-off for users who dismiss.
  • Cross-selling can feel like forced promotion.Require a real link of place, time or intent first.
08
Recommendation

So what's the call?

Fix reliability first.Make price predictable.Let discovery follow context.

  1. 1
    Prevent avoidable fulfillment failuresExtend existing delay messaging to other failure points, and pair each warning with a nearby alternative while the user can still act.
  2. 2
    Make the payable amount easy to understand before commitmentShow what the total is made of, why each charge exists, and what can change it.
  3. 3
    Connect ecosystem experiences contextuallyLink Food, Dineout and Scenes through moments of real intent, starting with one tested trigger.

The opportunity isn't to make Swiggy do more. It's to make every commitment feel more certain.

A
Appendix

What still needs validating?

Hypotheses, and how I'd test them

  1. Post-commit cancellations happen often enough to matter.Order data by failure stage and cause.
  2. A hard-to-read breakdown affects conversion or repeat use.Checkout funnel data and user interviews about the total.
  3. Some fulfillment failures are predictable from available signals.Back-test a simple model on historical orders.
  4. Users would act on a confidence signal.A limited A/B test measuring switches and outcomes.
  5. Better alternatives exist at the moment of risk.Depends on restaurant density, cuisine and time of day.
  6. Low-frequency surfaces lose attention to high-frequency ones.Cross-surface usage and home screen engagement data.
  7. Contextual triggers outperform static placement.One trigger tested against a navigation-only control.
  8. The archetypes reflect real behavior.Behavioral clustering and qualitative research.
  9. A points-based loyalty lever improves retention.A separate test, outside the transparency work.

Claims I deliberately avoided

  • Any Swiggy user counts, order volumes, revenue or growth figures
  • Actual cancellation, late-delivery, refund or missing-item rates
  • Specific fee amounts, or that prices or fees are “hidden”
  • That Swiggy has no delay-risk messaging (it already shows some)
  • Conversion or abandonment rates at checkout
  • That Scenes or Dineout are hidden, underused or underperforming
  • Which operational data Swiggy collects or how its systems work
  • Blame on restaurants or delivery partners for failures
  • Swiggy's internal segmentation, roadmap or strategy
